# TRTT Voyage Diary — Why I Am Fixing the Thinking Room Menu Before Publishing More News
**Date:** 10 November 2026  
**Status:** Draft for review — not published  
**Author’s note:** This is my working diary, not a breaking-news report.

## My note for today

For around fifteen days, I have spent a surprising amount of time on one thing: the Thinking Room menu. I know what that looks like from the outside. I could have published more news, prepared another travel article, or spent the time asking Google Search Console to index more URLs. Instead, I chose to work on the doorway through which a traveller may eventually reach the services and information I am trying to build.

I am not saying that news, search visibility or indexing are unimportant. They matter. But publishing more pages before the site’s navigation is dependable can leave readers with more content and no clear route through it. Today, my priority is to make that route work first.

## Why the menu matters to a traveller

A traveller rarely needs only one thing. A person planning a trip may need to return to the homepage, read a personal travel note, compare flights, arrange a ride, find an eSIM, explore a tour, or look at useful travel products. They may also want to understand who is behind the project.

The Thinking Room is intended to put those destinations in one recognisable place. The planned menu has eleven entries:

1. Home
2. My Diary
3. Flight
4. Ride
5. eSIM
6. Tour
7. Shop
8. Night
9. Thinking Room
10. ROKSANA LOCKED
11. About MAC

The point is not to decorate a page with eleven labels. Each item should have a clear destination or a clearly explained status. A locked item must remain visibly locked; it must not pretend to be a working service. The Thinking Room entry should open the menu, and the close button, X, Escape key and outside click should all let a visitor leave it easily.

## Why put Thinking Room inside the header menu?

A floating button can cover content, compete with other controls and feel disconnected from the site’s navigation. I want the Thinking Room button to sit inside the existing header menu, in the normal page flow, rather than hover over the page. That is why the launcher is being changed to static positioning.

This is a practical accessibility and usability decision: visitors should find the control where they expect navigation to be. It should not obstruct reading, cover a booking link, or jump around on a small phone screen.

## What will it control — and what will it not control?

I sometimes describe the menu as the control centre of TRTT Voyage. I mean that as a navigation and access point, not as a claim that one menu magically operates every external service.

The menu can bring the project’s routes together: the blog, diary, flight-search destination, ride information, eSIM resources, tours, shop and project information. When each route is properly connected, a traveller can move between them without hunting through unrelated pages.

The menu itself does not book flights, issue tickets, guarantee affiliate payouts, operate Amazon, or control GitHub and Vercel automatically. Those systems need their own working integrations, permissions and checks. The menu is the front door; the services behind each door must still work.

## Why I am not rushing to publish more news or request indexing

I want the blog to be useful after the first click. A large number of posts cannot compensate for confusing navigation or links that lead nowhere. For that reason, I have chosen to spend this period testing the structure before treating publishing volume as the main measure of progress.

Search Console indexing is not a switch that guarantees ranking or traffic. I will use it when the pages are ready, but I do not want to send readers or search engines toward a site experience that I have not finished checking. I also cannot promise that Google will consider any particular page high-quality simply because it is long or contains many links. The page must genuinely help its reader, be original, accurate, transparent and maintained.

## Why flight services and travel payouts still matter

I have not yet built a business around selling tickets directly. That is exactly why I am exploring a broader travel-support model instead of pretending that ticket sales already exist. Flight comparison can help a traveller discover available options; a ride service can help with the airport-to-destination leg; an eSIM can help with connectivity; and passenger-rights information can help people understand what to do when a flight is disrupted.

Some of these services may offer affiliate programmes. If a visitor chooses to use a partner through a marked link, the project may earn a commission, at no extra cost to the visitor in some programmes. A commission is never guaranteed, and I will disclose commercial links clearly. My first responsibility is to explain what a service does and let the traveller decide.

## Why Amazon is part of the plan

Travel does not begin and end with the flight ticket. People may need luggage, adapters, power banks, organisers or other practical travel equipment. A carefully selected shop section can make those items easier to find. Amazon links are one possible route for that section, but I will not claim that a product is personally tested unless I have actually tested it. Product descriptions, availability and prices can change, so readers should confirm details on the retailer’s page.

## Why I am not using GitHub to auto-publish everything

GitHub is where I keep and manage parts of the project’s code and working files. It is useful for version history and controlled updates. But a code repository should not automatically turn every file change into a public blog post. A menu update is not a news article, and an unreviewed automatic post could confuse readers.

For now, the process is deliberate: change the code, check the result, review the writing, and only then decide whether something is ready to publish. A draft is a draft. It is not evidence that the live site works.

## Why I am working with ChatGPT

I am using ChatGPT as a developer assistant, not handing it the keys to the whole project. I give the direction, set the limits and decide what is acceptable. The assistant can help inspect code, propose changes, organise notes and explain technical problems. It can also make mistakes. That is why I am keeping a diary and asking for visible evidence instead of accepting a confident “done” as proof.

I am not asking an AI system to invent travel facts, auto-publish content or claim that a feature works without testing. The final decision remains mine.

## What today’s menu work is meant to achieve

- Place the Thinking Room launcher inside the header menu.
- Keep the launcher in normal document flow with static positioning, not fixed and floating.
- Show the eleven agreed menu entries.
- Make the close button, X, Escape key and outside click close the overlay.
- Avoid changing the Blogger theme, adding a Gadget, restoring a theme or touching unrelated design work.
- Review the live result using a screenshot before calling the task complete.

This is a limited menu task. Colours, glow, inner visual redesign and extra features are deliberately postponed until the menu itself is checked.
